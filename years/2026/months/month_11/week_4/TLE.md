**TLE / Cookery — Preparation of Seafood, Classifications, and Composition**
- **Classifications of Fish & Seafood:**
  1. **Fin Fish:** Fish with fins and internal skeletons, usually covered with scales.
     - *External Anatomy of a Fin Fish (Activity):* Students were tasked to draw and label the external parts of a typical fin fish (25 points for external parts, 25 points for internal parts in the next activity):
       - **Fins:** Appendages used to maintain position, steer, stop, and maneuver in water. Includes single fins along the centerline (**dorsal fin** on the back, **caudal fin** / tail, **anal fin**) and **paired fins** (pelvic/ventral and pectoral). Catfish also have an **adipose fin** (fleshy lobe behind the dorsal fin).
       - **Scales:** Outer covering of bony fish (e.g., ganoid scales on catfish/gars).
       - **Gills:** Respiratory organs for breathing underwater.
       - **Eyes:** Can detect color; rounder lens structure than mammals to focus underwater.
       - **Nares (Nostrils):** Paired sensory organs used to detect odors in water (especially well-developed in eels and catfish).
       - **Lateral Line:** Sensory organ consisting of fluid-filled sacs with hair-like sensory apparatus running along the side of the fish to detect vibrations/movement in water.
       - **Vent:** External opening of the digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts.
  2. **Shellfish:** Soft-bodied invertebrates with external shells or jointed bodies/appendages.
     - **Crustaceans:** Have segmented bodies, jointed legs, and hard outer shells/exoskeletons covering the back/body and claws (e.g., shrimp, prawns, crabs, lobsters).
     - **Mollusks:** Soft-bodied shellfish classified by shell structure:
       - *Univalves:* Single shell (e.g., *kuhol* / snails).
       - *Bivalves:* Have a pair of hinged shells (e.g., clams, oysters, mussels / *tahong*, scallops).
       - *Cephalopods:* Have tentacles and no external shell (e.g., squid, octopus).
- **Composition and Structure of Fish:**
  - Fish consists of water, protein, fat, and small amounts of minerals and vitamins.
  - **Fat Content:** Varies by species (5% to 20% for fat fish; 2% to 5% for medium-fat fish; less than 2% for lean fish).
  - **Connective Tissue:** Fish has very little connective tissue compared to land animals, which makes it cook very quickly, become naturally tender even without long cooking times, and break apart easily when overcooked.
  - **Overcooking Effects:** Overcooking seafood (such as shrimp or squid) causes proteins to toughen and become rubbery. Moist-heat methods (like steaming) help preserve natural moisture and prevent drying out.
- **Fat Fish vs. Lean Fish:**
  - **Fat Fish:** High in fat and rich in Omega-3 fatty acids, which are beneficial for heart health (e.g., salmon, tuna, trout, mackerel).
  - **Lean Fish:** Low in fat (e.g., cod, sole, flounder, red snapper).
- **Water Sources:**
  - **Saltwater Fish:** Live in oceans/seas (e.g., tuna, mackerel, *maya-maya*, *lapu-lapu*).
  - **Freshwater Fish:** Live in ponds, lakes, and rivers (e.g., tilapia, catfish/*hito*, *bangus*/milkfish—note: *bangus* adapts to both/brackish water).
- **Market Forms of Fish:**
  - **Whole or Round:** Completely intact as caught.
  - **Drawn:** Viscera (internal organs) removed.
  - **Dressed:** Viscera, scales, head, tail, and fins removed.
  - **Steaks:** Cross-section slices cut from a large dressed fish, usually containing a section of the backbone.
  - **Fillets:** Boneless sides of the fish cut lengthwise away from the backbone.
  - **Butterflied Fillets:** Both sides of the fish still joined by the uncut flesh and skin of the back or belly.
  - **Sticks / Goujons:** Uniformly cut strips or pieces from fillets.
